# Channel brief — Enoch

Paste everything below the line into **Settings → Format → "Build this channel's format"**.

It is written as prose on purpose: the Format Analyst extracts structure from paragraphs,
and asking a model for a filled-in form invites it to invent field names. It answers all
six boxes, so it should come back with few or no gaps.

Derived from three transcripts of an existing channel in this niche — the repeating
machine, not that channel's content. Every episode-specific detail (four chambers, seven
heavens, the Antarctic structure) is deliberately absent; what is here is what recurred
across all three.

---

**1. Who is narrating.**

An archivist working through recovered documents, reading the record aloud and showing the
viewer what it actually says. Not a preacher, not a prophet, not a conspiracy host, not a
storyteller. Treats the viewer as a fellow researcher who is capable of drawing their own
conclusion, never as a congregation who needs to be told one. The narrator has no personal
stake and never testifies — they present. First person appears at most once in an entire
script, and only to concede a limit ("I am not saying this is a literal floor plan"), never
to assert.

**2. How this narrator reasons.**

Every claim is unpacked by the same move, without exception: point at a physical object or
a specific passage, say plainly what it contains, name who removed or restricted it and
when, then show what it resembles today — framed as resemblance, never as proof.

Two techniques carry that reasoning, and both are non-negotiable.

The first is **negation before assertion**. Clear away the expected reading, then land the
real one. "Not paradise, not reward. Rest and brightness." "Architecture, not vision, not
symbol." "Interpreted rather than translated." "Too precise, not too vague." This is the
default shape of any sentence that carries a claim.

The second is **one foreign or technical word per beat, unpacked against expectation**. Take
a single term from the source language, give its literal meaning, and show that the meaning
is more specific and stranger than the translation implies — the word for a cistern rather
than a prison, the word for water that cannot stop rather than crying, "dim" rather than
"dark". One word, opened up, does the emotional work of a whole paragraph of description.

Authorities are always characterised, never used to prove. The formula is: state what the
scholar did not say, then what they did. "He did not say it was false, he said it was
explosive." "Neither said it was untrue. Both said it was inconvenient." This grants the
claim weight without ever asserting it in the narrator's own voice.

Every abstraction is grounded in an occupational analogy — mundane, bureaucratic, physical.
The way a building inspector describes a building. The way a geologist's field notes read.
The way a librarian maintains a collection that was complete before they arrived. The way
governments guard classified intelligence. Cosmic material is always brought down to
somebody's job.

**3. Register and reading level.**

Serious, controlled and unhurried. Extraordinary claims are stated flatly — the weight comes
from the specificity, never from adjectives or from raising the voice.

Roughly twelfth-grade reading level. NOT eighth grade. Use the real vocabulary of the
subject — corpus, canon, scribal tradition, redaction, provenance, adjudicated — and define
a term in the same breath only when it genuinely is obscure. The felt sophistication comes
from proper-noun density more than from long words: name the exact catalogue number, the
exact year, the exact institution, and let those do the work.

Rhythm matters as much as vocabulary, and it is built on violent length contrast. A long
sentence carrying two nested clauses, then a fragment of three or four words landing alone.
"The case is open." "The prison is occupied." "The equipment was functioning." Never write
paragraph after paragraph of even, medium-length sentences — that flatness is the single
most recognisable failure in this genre.

Use triads for weight: "no administrative function, no prison, no garden." "Real dimensions,
real materials, real structure."

**4. What the narration must never do.**

- Never sermonise. No altar-call language, no "brothers and sisters", no Sunday-school
  gentleness, no moral instruction addressed to the viewer.
- Never claim proof. Say what the text says and lay facts adjacent; let the viewer draw the
  line. Never assert that something has been demonstrated, confirmed or established.
- Never accuse a living institution or a named living person of concealment. State what is
  restricted and state what it contains, next to each other, and stop there.
- Never use hype or clickbait phrasing — "you won't believe", "shocking", "they don't want
  you to know", "mind-blowing".
- Never invent a scientific finding, a study, a survey, or a scholar's opinion.
- Never write uniform medium-length sentences. See the rhythm rule above.
- Never open with a rhetorical question, a dictionary definition, or "imagine a world
  where".

**5. What every chapter does, in the same order, every time.**

1. Open on one physical thing the viewer could in principle stand in front of — a fragment,
   a manuscript page, a carved block, a sealed case. Say where it is and how big it is.
2. State plainly what it contains, in the flattest possible language.
3. Name who removed, restricted, softened or omitted it, and when. A body and a year.
4. Open the one word this chapter turns on, and show the gap between the translation and
   the original.
5. Show the modern echo — as resemblance only, never as corroboration — then close on a
   door that stays shut, and name the question the next chapter answers.

Across the whole video: the cold open runs under forty-five seconds and must pay off
something concrete within the first twenty. A new loop opens roughly every hundred seconds,
before the previous one has fully closed. The largest revelation is held back for the final
chapter and must be planned from the first — the video knows its ending before it starts.
The single first-person disclaimer goes immediately before the finale, conceding the
maximalist reading in order to earn the real one.

The video closes on a list of things that remain — the manuscript still in its church, the
fragment still under glass, the archive still closed — with one item in that list turning to
face the viewer's own present. Then it ends on waiting, not on a call to action, and it
returns to the object it opened on.

**6. What is on screen, and how it is treated.**

Manuscript surfaces, parchment fragments, carved stone, old maps, archive shelving, sealed
cases and locked rooms — the object itself, held still, never a dramatic re-enactment of the
event being described. When a place is named, show the place as it is now, not as it was.

Desaturated, low warm key light against deep shadow, aged paper texture, visible grain. Five
to eight seconds per image, slow push in or slow drift, no fast cuts and no whip transitions.

When a foreign word is unpacked, the word itself is on screen. When a year or a catalogue
number is spoken, it is on screen. The images carry the archive; the narration carries the
argument.
